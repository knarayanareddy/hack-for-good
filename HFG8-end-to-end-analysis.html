04 — ACAPS: Ground Truth, decoded

The brief asks a product question. The data answers a statistical one. Teams that only read the first will build a beautiful thing that cannot prove anything.

What is actually being asked

Strip the framing and the deliverable is a moderated, small-panel elicitation system with self-sovereign anonymity and a scoring model that survives six months of latency. Three constraints do most of the work:

  1. 5–10 experts per country. Not a platform. Not a community. A panel. This is the single most important number in the brief and it kills most growth-style product thinking.
  2. Minutes, not sessions. A contribution must be completable on a phone, on bad bandwidth, in a gap between meetings.
  3. Nobody is ever identifiable to the public. Not "we anonymise". Structurally cannot be attributed.

The brief then names two directions — ground truthing (Delphi-style reaction to ACAPS analyses) and forecasting (probability of resolvable events over six months) — and says the strongest solutions show how both feed one loop back into analysis, with outcomes shared publicly.

The insight hiding in the personas. Pierre "doubts the value of the effort" and Emily "goes elsewhere to check". Neither is a motivation problem — they are both evidence problems. The winning product does not gamify experts into contributing. It shows them, on entry, that the last time this panel spoke, the analysis changed. A visible change-log from expert input to published risk rating is worth more than any points, badges or streaks.

Data forensics: what the Risk Radar export actually supports

I parsed the provided export — data/risk-radar-export.csv, 139 rows × 25 columns. The headline claim in the brief ("roughly eight in ten High risks materialised, against about four in ten Low") is directionally right but needs care. Here is the honest version, computed on the 104 resolved risks:

High (n=38) 84.2%
Medium (n=58) 46.6%
Low (n=3) 33.3%

Materialisation rate among resolved risks, by ACAPS Risk Level. High vs Medium difference is statistically solid: z = 3.71. The Low bucket has only 3 resolved cases and cannot carry an argument — do not put it on a slide as if it means something.

The brief says "about four in ten of those rated Low". That is the headline from an earlier cut of the data; here Low resolved n=3. If you build a slide on the Low bucket you will be corrected by the challenge owner in the room. The Medium/High contrast is the defensible story.

Finding 1 — Probability carries the signal; Impact does not

Correlation with materialisation across resolved risks:

FieldCorrelation with "materialised"Reading
Probability (ordinal 1–5)0.348The predictive spine. High/Very High → 84.2%; Low/Very Low → 41.2%.
Impact (1–5)0.111Essentially noise. Impact is severity if it happens, not likelihood it happens.

This matters because "Risk Level" is a composite that is dominated by probability, and because a naive elicitation design will ask experts to estimate both dimensions in the same gesture. They are different questions with different resolution criteria. Ask them separately, score them separately. A design that asks "how risky is this?" produces an unfalsifiable answer and no track record.

Finding 2 — Big risks resolve; small risks disappear
>500,000 (n=46) 73.9%
250k–500k (n=11) 63.6%
100k–250k (n=7) 57.1%
20k–100k (n=16) 56.2%
<20,000 (n=21) 23.8%

Materialisation rate by expected exposure band, resolved risks. Small-scale risks appear far less likely to "materialise" — but this is at least partly observability, not reality. Nobody publishes when 8,000 people are affected the way they do when 800,000 are.

Design consequence: any scoring scheme that rewards "did it materialise" will systematically reward experts who predict large, visible, well-covered risks and punish those with granular local knowledge — exactly the people ACAPS most needs. Build in a difficulty adjustment (resolve-rate-normalised scoring, or Brier with per-question base rates) or you will optimise your panel into noise.

Finding 3 — There is no resolution date, and that breaks the forecast track record

The export has 25 columns and not one of them is a date field. The only temporal signal is free text: Rationale update, of which only 63 of 139 rows contain a parseable date. "What materialised?" is a prose description, not a timestamp.

The trap almost every ACAPS team will fall into

You cannot compute a genuine track record from this export, because a forecast score needs a question opened at T and resolved at T+6 months. The dataset tells you the outcome but not when it became known. Teams will nonetheless put a "superforecaster leaderboard" on the demo, populated by numbers they invented.

Two honest ways out, both of which score points: (a) reconstruct open/resolve dates with a documented, imperfect heuristic and show the uncertainty; or (b) ship the scoreboard as a shadow mode that runs on synthetic experts with a stated "resolves in N weeks" clock, and be explicit that real track records start after the pilot. Option (b) is faster and — because it is honest — reads as more senior.

Finding 4 — Only 7% of the corpus is even a forecasting problem

Risk types split 69 new-emerging / 56 deterioration / 8 future-impact-of-past-hazard. Only the middle category is naturally a "will this escalate" question with a clean resolution rule. The 28 active risks sit in 18 countries, with Sudan alone holding 6 — so a single-country panel in the pilot can be genuinely deep.

The demo is pre-chosen for you and it is good. R1043 — "Escalating cross-border insecurity linked to the Sudan conflict increases protection risks and humanitarian needs in eastern Chad." Active, High, probability High, impact 4, trend Deteriorated, exposure >500,000, priority needs Protection / Food security / Livelihoods / Health, with 4,128 characters of structured triggers and 3,568 characters of rationale. Chad also has a second active risk (R1066, spanning Chad–Niger–Cameroon–Nigeria). Build your entire narrative on Chad and you get a two-risk, 5–10-expert panel that is realistic in size, rich in data and directly named in the brief.

The concept that wins 40 points

The rubric gives Concept 40% — "is the concept a real solution for the challenge, and more than just an idea?" Translated: they are not grading your UI, they are grading whether you understood the problem. Most teams will build: a login, a risk card, a 1–5 slider, a comment box, a chart. That is a survey. Here is the version that is not.

Three design moves that make it a concept

  1. Anonymity as a capability, not a checkbox. Three tiers — known to ACAPS / pseudonymous to peers / fully anonymous — are the minimum, and the brief names them. The concept step is making the tier legible in the aggregate: a moderator sees "3 of 7 experts disagree, 2 of them fully anonymous", never who. Ship a visible attribution test in the demo: one click that shows exactly what the public output can and cannot reveal, computed live. Nothing earns trust from a safety-minded jury faster than proving the negative.
  2. Disagreement as the product. The brief asks "how do you aggregate without creating groupthink?" The lazy answer is an average. The correct answer is that consensus is the wrong target for risk analysis — a 5–10 panel that agrees early has stopped adding information. Show the spread and the dissent, and route the outlier to the analyst as the most valuable object in the system. A panel of 7 where one expert says "you are wrong about Chad" is worth more than 7 votes of 60%.
  3. The loop must be visible, or nobody comes back. Every expert contribution should end with a promise and a receipt: "This fed into the R1043 update on 14 Dec. Here is what changed." That single feature is the entire answer to the brief's stated problem that comparable forecasting pools lose participation after launch. It is also 20 lines of UI and 40 points of narrative.

05 — UNICRI: Stop Before It Starts, decoded

This brief is a distribution problem wearing an engineering costume. It says so twice, plainly: “Focus on making adoption cheap, easy and compelling, not on building new detection.”

What is actually being asked

Three building blocks, one package. The message and the route to help are given to you, with sources. The creative and engineering space is entirely in the third block: the plug-and-play integration. The target is explicit and it is the whole design brief compressed into one sentence: a Dutch hobby forum with 20,000 members and one part-time moderator who wants this live within a week.

Provided — don't rebuild
  • data/help-services.json — 5 verified Dutch/global services with confidentiality notes
  • data/message-blocks.json — NL + EN blocks, each cited to evidence
  • data/legal-confidentiality.json — per-country legal and confidentiality
  • contract/PLUGIN_CONTRACT.md + JSON schemas
  • mock-platform/ — runnable local fake platform
  • tools/generate-traffic.js — synthetic traffic generator
  • integrations/ — Discourse and WordPress examples
Rules that constrain your build
  • Only test-trigger-* placeholders. Never real abuse-related terms, anywhere — code, comments, commits, docs.
  • No query text passed to the module. The module knows a trigger fired, not what was typed.
  • sessionScopedId is random, per session, never persisted.
  • Aggregate measurement only, counts suppressed below 10.
  • No fingerprinting, no IP logging, no cross-session IDs.
  • npm run check enforces this — run it in CI, and say so on stage.

The measurement math nobody will do

The brief cites two anchors: roughly 1 in 100 people who see a warning click through to help, and a dark-web field experiment where redesigning messages lifted click-through from 8.7% to 15.7%. Put those next to the suppression rule and a hard constraint appears:

To detect a doubling of click-through (1% → 2%) at 80% power, α = 0.05 two-sided, you need ≈ 2,300 impressions per arm — about 4,600 in total.

A 25% relative improvement (1% → 1.25%) needs on the order of 46,000 impressions. A 20,000-member hobby forum will not produce that in a week.

And because counts under 10 are suppressed, a platform with 900 impressions and 6 clicks sees nothing — the dashboard shows a dash, not a signal. Your impact dashboard is structurally blind at exactly the scale your target customer operates.

This is the highest-value insight in the UNICRI track, and almost no team will surface it. The honest answer is not to fake statistical power — it is to redesign what "impact" means at small scale: sequential testing so you can stop early, Bayesian reporting with credible intervals rather than point rates, an exposure metric (impressions shown) decoupled from outcome metrics, and per-install expectations set at "we will know in 6 weeks" rather than "here is today's conversion rate". Say the word power analysis out loud in your pitch and you will separate from the room.

Where the concept points are

  1. The install path is the product. A moderator's decision is risk, not effort. Your landing surface should be a five-minute <script> tag, a snippet for Discourse, and a page that says precisely what data does and does not leave their server. Ship the copy-paste, not the architecture diagram.
  2. Habituation is a design constraint, not an afterthought. The brief asks explicitly what happens on someone's fifth sighting. A static interstitial trains dismissal. Escalating intervention ladders — awareness → consequence → route to help — with a hard cap on repetition, is a real concept.
  3. Honesty is the conversion mechanism. Fear of being reported is the stated reason people don't seek help, and confidentiality rules differ by country. A per-country, honestly-worded confidentiality statement — especially where a reporting duty exists, as in England under the Crime and Policing Act 2026 — is what makes the click safe. Being clear that you are not promising confidentiality where the law does not give it is a trust feature, not a legal footnote.
  4. The message A/B harness should be visible in the demo. The brief wants "design for testing variants". Show a variant registry with cited evidence per block. That converts your pitch from "a warning" to "an evidence pipeline".

Three rubric exploits that are entirely legitimate

  • Read the criteria out loud in your pitch. "You asked whether the concept is more than an idea — here is the mechanism, and here is it running." Judges score against a visible sheet; naming the criterion as you satisfy it is not gaming, it is clarity.

09 — For the organisers: eight fixable defects

Included because an end-to-end analysis of the page that stops at the participant's side is only half the job. All are observable from outside the auth wall.

  1. The submission deadline is inconsistent by one hour. Public schedule says 12:00 CET; submissionsEndTime is 11:00 CET. Teams reading the schedule will miss the form. This is the only defect on this list that can directly cost a team their weekend.
  2. Team size contradicts itself. Description says 2–6; maxTeamSize is 5. A sixth member literally cannot join.
  3. No pre-registration view of challenges. The gate is applied to /participate/* globally, so an unregistered visitor — a recruiter, a journalist, a judge — cannot see the two challenges. A public, read-only challenge preview would cost nothing and would have made this analysis unnecessary.
  4. The 404 gate is edge-cached for ~20 hours. Signed-out users get a stale 404 with no sign-in prompt, which reads as a broken link rather than an auth wall. A 302 to /auth/signin is the conventional pattern and Clerk supports it natively.
  5. Challenges are stripped even from the public event payload ("challenges":[]), so the public event page cannot list its own tracks. The information architecture is inverted: the least discoverable page carries the most important decision.
  6. No resolution-date field in the ACAPS export. 25 columns and no timestamp means no team can build a true forecast track record, which is a headline feature of the brief. Adding identified_date and resolved_date would materially raise the ceiling of every submission.
  7. The "Low" bucket in the brief does not survive contact with the data. The brief cites ~4-in-10 Low materialisation; the shipped export has 3 resolved Low risks. Either ship a larger cut or adjust the claim — teams will build slides on it and be corrected on stage.
  8. No stated submission format enforcement. Both briefs require prototype + 3-minute pitch + repo with description, but nothing on the platform indicates how that is captured or judged. A one-line template on the participate page would raise the floor of every submission.

Method.
