00 — What unlimited time actually changes

With a weekend, the question is "what can we finish." With unlimited time, the question becomes much more dangerous and much more interesting: "what is actually true?"

Almost every hackathon project is built on an untested assumption dressed as a premise. For this brief the premise is: a small panel of experts, given a well-designed interface, will produce forecasts that beat what ACAPS's public-source analysis already achieves. Nobody in the room will have checked that. Most submissions will assume it, build a beautiful interface on top of it, and hope.

The winning strategy under unlimited time is to make that assumption the thing you test first. Not because rigour is virtuous, but because of what happens to your pitch if you can say: "Before we asked a single expert for their time, we built the benchmark their forecasts must beat — and here it is, measured on 104 resolved ACAPS risks." That sentence is worth more than any feature you could build in the same hours. It converts your project from a proposal into a finding.

Three things change concretely when the clock stops mattering:

DimensionWeekend versionUnlimited-time version
Order of workBuild → demo → (never) validate.Validate → build only what the validation licenses → validate again with real users.
What the prototype provesThat the UI renders and the buttons work.That the mechanism produces measurably better forecasts than the expert-free baseline, on real historical outcomes.
What you are actually deliveringA demo.A pilot-ready instrument, a validity dossier, a trust model, and a costed operating plan.

The rubric rewards this disproportionately. Concept is 40% — "a real solution, and more than just an idea." A team that arrives with a measured result and a falsifiable design is not competing on the same axis as a team with a nicer gradient.

01 — The scientific foundation

I ran the numbers on the supplied risk-radar-export.csv (139 risks, 104 resolved) to find out what BENCHMARK actually has to beat. These four results shape every design decision that follows.

The logistic benchmark — four structured fields only (probability, impact, risk type, exposure band), zero human input — reaches an in-sample Brier of 0.1981. Cross-validated it will be worse, but the direction is the point: most of the predictive work is already done by structured metadata. This is the bar. An expert panel that produces a Brier of 0.20 has produced nothing, and a pitch that does not know this is a pitch that cannot answer the first hard question a statistically literate judge will ask.

Result 3 — Aggregation arithmetic is probably not the differentiator

Mean0.2196
Median0.2215

Simulated 7-person panels (anchor + independent log-odds noise), 300 draws, scored against real outcomes. All four rules land within 0.004 Brier of each other.

Honest caveat, stated up front: this simulation assumes panelists bring the anchor plus noise — no new information. Under that assumption no aggregation rule can help, so the result is closer to a tautology than a discovery. But it points at the right priority: the arithmetic is second-order; the information is first-order. Competitors will spend their time on extremising and log-odds pooling. We will spend ours on elicitation quality, panel calibration and resolution discipline — and we will test this properly in experiment E2 rather than assuming it.

Result 4 — The trigger structure is a hidden question bank with a built-in baseline

The Triggers field is structured, not prose: 108 of 139 risks carry 266 trigger blocks, each with an ID, title, description, its own sources, and a Completion rate percentage (mean 52.6%, 12 triggers at 100%). R1043 carries three: expansion of cross-border attacks (40%), attacks on strategic infrastructure (10%), renewed proxy mobilisation (20%). 76 triggers sit on live active risks.

This is the single most important asset in the brief and almost nobody will notice it. It gives BENCHMARK three things at once: a ready-made question bank, a natural unit of expert work (one trigger, four minutes), and — critically — an expert-derived baseline to beat on every single question. That baseline is what makes the whole system honest: an expert who simply copies ACAPS scores exactly zero skill, by construction.

The asymmetry that decides it

UNICRI's challenge is downstream of evidence you cannot generate: whether a warning changes behaviour depends on real people in a real moment, and you are forbidden from touching them. So every UNICRI team — including a very good one — is pitching an untested hypothesis with a nice interface.

ACAPS's challenge lets you settle the core empirical question offline, in a weekend, against 104 real outcomes. That is an enormous asymmetry in what "more than just an idea" can mean. Take the track where reality can mark your homework.

If you have the people to run two five-person pods (the platform permits it: allowMultipleSubmissions: true, maxTeamSize: 5), run BENCHMARK as primary and OFFRAMP as a hedge — §15 is the full blueprint. With fewer than eight people, run BENCHMARK only. Two half-projects lose to one whole one.

03 — The product: the closed loop

BENCHMARK is a forecasting instrument, not a survey. The difference is that every object in it eventually resolves, scores, and reports back. Twelve steps, and the loop is closed at both ends — expert to analysis, and analysis back to expert.

Ingest

Risk Radar export + the live Risk List via the ACAPS API (token auth) or HDX. Risks, triggers, ratings, histories, outcomes.

Decompose

Each risk becomes N resolvable questions, one per trigger. "Will attacks on strategic infrastructure in Chad occur before 30 April 2027?" beats "how risky is R1043?" — it is answerable, scoreable, and takes ninety seconds.

Anchor

Every question inherits a baseline: the trigger's Completion rate, plus the historical materialisation rate for risks of that type, exposure band and probability. This is the feature nobody else will have. The panel is never asked for a number in a vacuum.

Elicit — round 1

Independent, blind, four minutes on a phone. Probability (five taps) + one optional sentence of reasoning. No visibility of anyone else's view. Language and bandwidth negotiated, not assumed.

Aggregate, visibly

Distribution, not a point estimate. Spread, quartiles, and the distance from the baseline. A signal that says more than the mean ever could: "the panel is 22 points above ACAPS and two standard deviations apart from each other."

Deliberate — round 2

Anonymised summary of round 1 plus the reasons attached to outlier positions. Experts may revise. The delta between rounds is measured — that number is the brief's groupthink question, answered empirically rather than asserted.

Dissent routing

The outlier is promoted, not averaged away. A moderator receives "1 of 7 experts, fully anonymous, dissents strongly — here is the reasoning." This is usually the most valuable object the system produces.

Synthesise

Moderator copilot drafts a rationale in ACAPS house style from the contributions. Every generated sentence is clickable back to the contribution that produced it. It refuses to generate where unsupported. AI stays behind the glass.

Publish

Aggregate view and dissent summary enter the analyst workflow and, where appropriate, the public Risk List. Individual contributions are never attributable — enforced by the anonymity engine, not by policy.

Resolve

At the horizon, a structured resolution: criterion, evidence, date, verdict. Two independent resolvers plus an escalation path. Resolution is a first-class object with an owner and a deadline — the thing the dataset is missing.

Score

Brier skill relative to the baseline, calibration curve, and a difficulty adjustment so that a correct call on a 10% trigger counts more than a correct call on a 90% one. Panel weights update with shrinkage and are capped so no single expert can capture the aggregate.

Receipt

Every panelist is told what their contribution changed and how their call resolved. The brief's stated retention failure — comparable forecasting pools lose participation after launch — is answered here, and nowhere else.

04 — The five hard problems, solved

These are the questions the brief actually asks. Everything else is interface.

1 · How does an expert choose and change their anonymity, and how do you guarantee nothing is attributed publicly?

Three tiers — known to ACAPS, pseudonymous to peers, fully anonymous — selectable and changeable at any time, with retroactive effect on anything not yet published. The guarantee is not a promise; it is a publication gate.

  • The Anonymity Budget Engine computes, for every proposed aggregate output, whether any individual contribution can be inferred — from small cells, from cross-round differencing between consecutive publications, from attribute combinations, from timing correlation.
  • If the answer is yes, the system refuses to publish and explains why. A refusal is a feature you can demo in ten seconds and which no competitor will have.
  • Free text is paraphrased and merged before publication, because writing style is an identifier. Stylometric risk is in the threat model, not ignored.
  • Publication timing is staggered across panels so that a change in one country cannot be dated against a known ACAPS cycle.
  • Threat model, stated honestly: we defend against a motivated reader holding all public outputs. We do not claim protection against a state actor with legal compulsion over ACAPS, and we say so in the product, not the appendix. A scoped claim is more credible than an absolute one.

2 · How do you aggregate without creating groupthink?

  • Independence is structural: round 1 is blind, enforced in the data model rather than the UI. A panelist physically cannot see another's number before committing their own.
  • The dissent router inverts the objective. Consensus is not the goal; a 7-person panel that agrees in round one has stopped adding information. The product surfaces spread and promotes the outlier to the analyst.
  • Opinion change is instrumented. Round-1→round-2 deltas are recorded. If convergence is high and accuracy does not improve, the protocol is wrong and experiment E7 tells you so — you adjust (blind round 2, dialectical bootstrapping) instead of shipping the flaw.
  • Anchoring is measured. Distance-from-baseline per panelist, tracked over time. Chronic anchor-hugging is visible and carries exactly zero scoring benefit.

3 · How do you turn ACAPS risks into questions that can actually be resolved and scored after six months?

This is the question that separates a real submission from a demo, and the dataset makes it hard on purpose: there are no date fields anywhere in the export. So resolution becomes a product rather than a side-effect.

  • Two resolution templates cover the corpus: trigger-occurs (did the described hazard happen by date D, evidenced by ≥2 independent sources) and threshold-crossed (did exposure/displacement exceed X by date D). Templates are versioned and shown to the expert at forecast time — you cannot forecast honestly without knowing how it will be judged.
  • Resolution has an owner and a deadline. A queue, an SLA, and an escalation path. Nothing silently expires.
  • Two independent resolvers for contested cases, with a documented tie-break. Resolution quality is itself measured: if a question proves unresolvable, that is logged as a design failure and the template changes.
  • Retrospective backfill. For the 104 already-resolved risks, we reconstruct approximate resolution dates from the Rationale update text (63/139 contain a parseable date) and mark them date_confidence: low. Used for backtesting only, never shown to experts as authoritative.

4 · How does ACAPS moderate this without a heavy workload?

  • The panel is deliberately small: 5–10 per country. Moderation is a 20-minute weekly ritual per country, not a community management job.
  • The copilot drafts, the moderator approves or edits. Every draft is cited. Generation is refused when evidence is thin — which is most of the time, and that is correct behaviour.
  • Exception-based workflow: the moderator's queue contains only what needs a human — a strong dissent, a resolution conflict, a stale question. Everything routine is automatic.
  • Load is measured and published internally: minutes per country per week is a KPI with a target, not an afterthought.

5 · Why would a busy expert come back?

The brief states the failure mode precisely: comparable pools lose participation after launch. Three mechanisms, in order of importance:

  1. The Receipt. Every contribution ends with proof of consequence. "Your call on T0618 fed the 14 December update. R1043 moved from High/40% to High/58%." This is the answer, and it is cheap.
  2. Four minutes, honestly enforced. A completion budget is a product constraint: if a flow takes longer, it is redesigned. Measured in production, on real devices, on real connections.
  3. Recognition without exposure. A calibration percentile, visible only to the panelist, never a public ranking of named experts. The brief lists a super-estimator scoreboard as a could; done badly it destroys the trust the product depends on. Done as private percentile bands, it retains without exposing.

06 — Phase plan

Eight phases. Each has an exit criterion that is a testable claim, not a status update. Nothing advances on schedule pressure; phases advance on evidence.

P0

Foundations & the benchmark

Weeks 1–3

Before any interface: build the measurement harness. Parse the Risk Radar export into clean Risk/Trigger records. Construct the baseline benchmark (base rate, ACAPS probability, structured logistic) with proper cross-validation. Define the scoring spec in writing — Brier skill, difficulty adjustment, panel weights — and circulate it for criticism before building anything that assumes it.

Exit: a documented benchmark number that any expert panel must beat, computed out-of-sample, reproducible from a script in the repo.
P1

Question engine & resolution protocol

Weeks 3–6

Trigger → question generation with the two templates. Resolution criterion DSL, versioned. Retrospective date reconstruction for backtesting. A public "how questions resolve" page, because experts must be able to see the rules.

Exit: 40 historical questions reconstructed with resolution dates and criteria, agreeing with the recorded outcome in ≥90% of cases.
P2

The elicitation loop

Weeks 5–11

Round 1 blind capture, aggregation with visible spread, round 2 with reasons and delta capture. The four-minute flow, tested on a real phone on a throttled connection. Dissent routing. The shape of this is the product; build it properly once.

Exit: median completion under four minutes with five internal testers and one domain expert; round deltas captured and queryable.
P3

Anonymity engine

Weeks 6–14

Publication gate, small-cell suppression, differencing protection, stylometry filter, timing stagger. Build the red-team suite first and make the engine pass it — an attack suite you wrote is worth more than a policy you wrote.

Exit: automated red-team re-identifies zero synthetic panelists from published outputs across 10,000 simulated publications. Attacks and results both published in the repo.
P4

Validity: does the panel actually add information?

Weeks 8–20

The existential test (E3 below). Recruit 20–30 humanitarian experts through the ACAPS network and beyond. Have them forecast 40 already resolved questions, blind to outcomes. Measure incremental Brier over the baseline. This is the study that decides whether BENCHMARK is a forecasting instrument or an expert-enrichment inbox, and either answer is a strong finding.

Exit: a published result — positive or negative — on whether a 7-person panel beats the structured baseline. Pre-register the analysis before collecting data.
P5

Pilot with ACAPS

Weeks 16–28

One country, one panel of 5–10, one live risk set. Chad is the natural choice: two active risks, three triggers on R1043, >500k exposed, and named in the brief. Two full cycles — open, elicit, publish, resolve, receipt — inside eight weeks.

Exit: one panel through two complete cycles; ≥70% week-8 retention; ≥1 documented case where expert input changed a published rating, with the analyst's sign-off.
P6

Harden & scale

Weeks 26–38

Multi-country. Additional languages (French, Arabic, Spanish). Moderator tooling for five simultaneous panels. Cost per country per month measured and reduced. The operational documentation that lets ACAPS run it without the founding team in the room.

Exit: three countries operating concurrently; moderator time under 20 minutes per country per week; runbook complete enough for a handover.
P7

Sustainability

Weeks 30–44

Funding model, sector replication (the same instrument works for any organisation running expert panels on slow-moving risk), and the public outcomes layer the brief asks for — where resolved questions and their results are visible to everyone, including the experts who made them.

Exit: a funding commitment covering ≥12 months of operations, and a public outcomes page with ≥50 resolved questions.

07 — The Research Programme

Seven experiments, each designed to be able to fail. This is the machinery that makes winning probable rather than lucky: you will know which claims are true before you make them.

IDQuestionDesignWhat it changes if we are wrong
E1What exactly must we beat? Base rate, ACAPS probability, and a 4-field logistic model, all with k-fold CV on the 104 resolved risks. Sets the target. If the logistic model beats experts-in-prospect, we reframe the product as enrichment, not forecasting.
E2Does aggregation arithmetic matter? Ablation: mean, median, trimmed mean, log-odds, extremised, and surprisingly popular (ask belief and belief-about-others). Scored on historical simulation and on E3's real panel data. If a rule wins clearly, we adopt it. If not, we stop spending engineering time on it and say so — a credible negative result.
E3Do experts actually add information? Pre-registered. 20–30 experts × 40 resolved questions, blind. Primary outcome: incremental Brier skill over the baseline, with bootstrap CIs. Everything. This is the existential test. A null result pivots the product to "expert enrichment and dissent capture" — still valuable, honestly positioned, and far more defensible than an unsupported forecasting claim.
E4Can the anonymity engine be broken? Automated red-team: elimination, differencing across rounds, attribute combination, stylometry, timing correlation, 10,000 simulated publications. If it breaks, we tighten thresholds and re-run. If it cannot be tightened without destroying utility, that is a fundamental design finding and we publish it.
E5Does the Receipt drive retention? Within-panel A/B over 6 weeks: receipt vs. no receipt, measured on week-4 and week-12 participation. If no effect, retention needs a different mechanism (recognition, direct ask, cadence). Given 6 weeks, a within-panel A/B is weak — flagged, and run as a stepped-wedge instead.
E6Is four minutes real? Instrumented field test: 10 experts, real devices, throttled 3G, French and English. If median > 4 min, the flow is redesigned before pilot. Non-negotiable constraint, not a stretch goal.
E7Does the panel groupthink? Measure round-1→round-2 convergence and whether convergence predicts accuracy. Compare blind-round-2 vs. visible-summary-round-2. If convergence is large and uninformative, we change the protocol — a genuine protocol contribution rather than a UI tweak.

Why this programme is itself the winning move. Every competitor will assert that expert panels improve analysis. You will have tested it, with a pre-registered design, on real outcomes, and be able to say what you found — including the parts that did not work. That is what "more than just an idea" means when a jury has to choose between four dashboards and one instrument that knows its own error bars.

08 — Trust engineering

Anonymity is the brief's stated non-negotiable, and the most common way to fail it is to treat it as a settings screen. Here it is a subsystem with its own tests.

The threat model, in writing

  • Adversary: motivated reader holding all published outputs, plus general knowledge of who works where in a country's humanitarian sector.
  • Not defended against: ACAPS insiders, state compulsion over the hosting organisation, global traffic analysis. Stated in the product.
  • Assets: which expert said what, and their numeric forecasts across rounds.
  • Attack surface: small cells, round-to-round differencing, attribute combinations, writing style, publication timing.

Controls, mapped to attacks

  • Small cells → k-anonymity gate, publish-or-refuse.
  • Differencing → correlated-noise publication across consecutive releases.
  • Attributes → combination suppression on quasi-identifiers (role × country × tenure).
  • Style → paraphrase and merge free text before publication.
  • Timing → staggered, batched publication per country.
  • All of it → an automated red-team suite that must pass before any release.

The demo moment this buys you: a judge adds a hypothetical second expert to a cell and watches the system refuse to publish, with a plain-language reason — "with two experts in eastern Chad, one of whom is fully anonymous, the anonymous view is identifiable by elimination." Ten seconds, and no other team in the room can do it.

15 — The parallel track: OFFRAMP

If you have eight to ten people, run a second five-person pod on UNICRI. The platform permits it (allowMultipleSubmissions: true, maxTeamSize: 5), and two strong submissions in two tracks dominate one strong submission in one. Below is the compressed blueprint.

Concept — "OFFRAMP": a plug-and-play prevention package that treats the warning as a handover, not a poster. Three claims nobody else will make: that the intervention escalates across repeat encounters rather than repeating; that the promise of confidentiality is honest per jurisdiction, including where the honest answer is "we cannot promise you that"; and that impact is measured in a way that refuses to report when the sample cannot support a claim.

OFFRAMP's research programme (shorter, because the evidence base is external): a synthetic A/B harness on the message ladder; a habituation simulation at realistic volume; a resolvability test of the suppression rule at n=500/900/5,000; and a red-team of the plugin contract to prove the module cannot see query text. All are runnable without real people, which is why they are permissible. Sequencing: BENCHMARK first, always. OFFRAMP's starter kit means a competent five-person pod can reach a strong submission from a standing start — it is the better hedge precisely because it needs less runway. Do not let it borrow BENCHMARK's people during P0–P2.

Cadence

  • Weekly: one decision review. Only one thing is decided — what to stop. Everything else is status.
  • Fortnightly: a research review where the experiments report, including failures. Failures are agenda items, not embarrassments.
  • Monthly: a pilot review against the four pilot success criteria, with authority to pause expansion.
  • Always: a written decision log. Every irreversible choice — protocol, threat model scope, scoring rule — with the reasoning and the date. This artefact is also, conveniently, the strongest possible pitch material.
