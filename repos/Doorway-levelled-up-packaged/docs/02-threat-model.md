# Doorway — threat model

*What the product refuses to do, why each refusal is load-bearing, and what an attacker gains if a refusal is dropped.*

This document exists because a coverage check against the brief found that one of my own recommendations broke a stated safety rule. Refusals written down are refusals that survive a delivery deadline.

---

## R1 · No session identifier. No user identifier. No device fingerprint.

**What we do instead.** Everything that requires knowing *this person* lives in `localStorage` on their device and never crosses a network boundary. What leaves is eight fields, none of which is unique to an individual.

**Why.** A random, session-scoped identifier — which the organisers' own `PLUGIN_CONTRACT.md` specifies — stops being anonymous the moment it appears twice. Two events sharing an ID link those events; three sessions sharing an install ID link the sessions. The kit's privacy note is accurate that the ID is never persisted; the aggregation layer nevertheless holds it for the window in which linkage happens.

**What an attacker gains if dropped.** Anyone with access to the aggregate store can reconstruct a browsing history for a person, and the population is people who searched for abuse material. That is a list of suspects, generated by a child-protection tool. There is no version of this that is acceptable.

---

## R2 · No query text, ever.

**The module receives a boolean, not a string.** The platform decides a trigger fired; Doorway is told *that* it fired and never *what* was typed.

**Why it costs us something.** With the query we could pick a message, personalise the route, and be more helpful. Every design instinct says take the string. We do not, because a string is content, content is evidence, and evidence is subpoenable.

**What an attacker gains if dropped.** A store of abuse-related search terms, keyed to time and platform. There is no safe way to hold that.

---

## R3 · No redirect through our infrastructure.

The help link goes directly to Offlimits with `?src=doorway`. Nothing of ours is in the path.

**Why.** A redirect through our own domain would let us measure arrival precisely — the single most valuable metric in the product. It would also log the IP address of every person who asked for help. **That trade is refused; we take the imprecision instead.**

---

## R4 · No public trigger lists.

Packs are curated privately, distributed as signed bundles to verified installs, and never committed to the public repository. Cloudflare edge mode means the list does not enter the platform's own repository either.

**Why.** The brief is explicit: publishing exactly what triggers a warning teaches people to avoid it. A community-maintained pack — which an earlier version of my own advice recommended — is a public trigger list with extra steps.

**What an attacker gains if dropped.** A published map of what to avoid, which converts the intervention into an evasion guide.

---

## R5 · No classification model, no scores, no propensity estimates.

**Why.** Three separate lines of the brief forbid it: *"Should not: identify, track or expose individuals"*; *"Must: protect the privacy of the people who see it"*; safety rule 2, *"nothing that identifies, exposes or tracks individuals."*

**And a practical reason that matters more.** The best predictor of escalation in the literature is frequency of use — and frequency is a counter, not a classifier. A model adds risk and no signal.

**What an attacker gains if dropped.** A ranking of people by likelihood of offending, built on data from a tool they were told could not identify them. This is the single fastest way to destroy the product's reason to exist.

---

## R6 · No counselling chatbot.

Buttons, then a human. reThink's own evaluation found its chatbot handled free text badly — 132 of ~1,650 conversations were analysable and 80% of users preferred buttons. A hackathon chatbot would be worse, and therapy-adjacent copy is a liability.

---

## R7 · No assumption of intent in the copy.

The classifier cannot tell a journalist, a survivor, a parent, a researcher or an accidental hit from an offender. So no message may say "you are looking for this." Every message addresses the material and the law, not the person's character.

**The gap this exposes.** No deployment in the literature has a false-positive path — a way for a platform to report a bad block and get the list changed. A full product needs one. See `03-the-gap-to-real.md`.

---

## R8 · No real people, no live-platform testing.

Every demonstration runs on the mock platform. No contact with at-risk people or children, no decoy profiles, no posting in perpetrator forums, and **no abuse-related search terms typed anywhere, including in generated examples.**

Not negotiable, not even for a screenshot.

---

## The one-line test

If a proposed feature requires knowing which person did something, it is out — regardless of how much better it would make the product. That rule is simple enough to apply at 3 a.m. on Sunday, which is the only time such a rule matters.
