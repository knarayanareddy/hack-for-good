01 — How to read the direction cards

Each direction is scored against the published rubric. The number in the corner is projected points out of 100 if executed competently — not a prediction of winning, a comparison of ceiling. Two warnings about that number:

  • Differences of 2–3 points are noise. Nothing separates an 81 from an 84 in a room with three tired judges. Differences of 8+ are real and worth choosing on.
  • A high ceiling you cannot ship scores zero. A 71 you finish beats an 86 you demo half-built. That is why every card carries a separate build-risk tag.
40%
Concept
Won in the first 60 seconds. Pure mechanism quality — independent of code.
20%
Prototype
"Does it work?" Narrow and running beats broad and rendered.
15%
Tech feasibility
Ethics, security, legal side of AI. Free points both briefs hand you.

Tags used throughout: low risk ship it comfortably · medium needs discipline · high only with a strong builder · bundle designed to combine.

The structural insight that shapes everything below. Both briefs are secretly about the same three things: trust you can prove, a loop that closes, and a constraint you respect rather than work around. ACAPS asks it as anonymity; UNICRI asks it as privacy-preserving measurement. Every direction that scores above 80 is a variation on one of those three. Every direction that scores in the 70s is a feature.

02 — ACAPS: seven directions

The brief supplies a 139-risk corpus with real outcomes and 266 distinct triggers. Almost every team will treat it as a backdrop. It is the product.

New in this pass: I pulled the Triggers field apart. It has structure nobody mentions — 108 of 139 risks carry 266 trigger blocks, each with an ID (R1043 - T0618), a title, a description, its own source URLs, and a Completion rate percentage (mean 52.6%; 12 triggers at 100%). R1043 alone has three: expansion of cross-border attacks (40%), attacks on strategic infrastructure (10%), renewed proxy mobilisation (20%).

That is a ready-made question bank with an existing expert-derived baseline to beat — and it is the foundation of the strongest direction on this page. Ask ACAPS on Friday exactly what "completion rate" means (confidence the trigger will fire, or progress toward it). The whole of A1 depends on the answer, and it is a 30-second question.

A1

Beat the Baseline — trigger-level elicitation

Ask experts to forecast individual triggers, not whole risks. Score them against ACAPS's own completion rate.

86
projected
Thesis
Asking a field expert to rate "the risk level of R1043" is asking them to do an analyst's job. Asking them "will attacks on strategic infrastructure in Chad happen in the next six months — 10% today, what do you say?" is asking them something they actually know. Decompose the risk into its triggers and the cognitive load collapses.
Why it scores
Concept is near-maximum: it is a real mechanism, visibly different from a survey, and it uses the supplied data at a depth no other team will reach. Prototype is genuinely small — one card, one number. Tech feasibility is free because the scoring is defensible arithmetic.
Build
Trigger card stack (one per screen, mobile-first) → probability input (5 taps) → panel distribution → Brier vs the ACAPS baseline → "triggers you moved most" summary for the moderator.
Hard part
Honest scoring. A trigger at 40% baseline that fires is not a win for someone who said 45%. Score against the baseline, not against 50%, or you reward herd behaviour.
Demo moment
"ACAPS rates T0618 at 40%. Our seven Chad experts say 72% — and here are the three whose last twelve calls beat the baseline."
Pilot fit
Excellent. 76 triggers sit on live active risks — Chad alone gives a 3-trigger, 7-expert pilot that ships in December.
low build riskstatisticalbundle with A2
A2

The Receipt — a loop that visibly closes

Every contribution returns proof of what it changed. The retention product, not the elicitation product.

78
projected
Thesis
The brief states the failure mode plainly: comparable forecasting pools lose participation after launch. The cause is never the UI — it is that contributors never learn whether they mattered. Build the evidence layer that makes contribution feel like it went somewhere.
Why it scores
Answers a stated problem rather than an inferred one, which judges reward. Cheap to build, high narrative value. Lower ceiling than A1 because on its own it is a layer, not a system.
Build
Contribution ledger → risk-rating diff ("the panel moved R1043 from High/40% to High/58%") → resolution card ("you said 72%; it fired; you were in the top 3 of 9") → notification surface.
Hard part
You need a plausible upstream. A receipt that says "thanks!" is worse than none. Show the actual diff on the actual rating.
Demo moment
An expert opens the app after eight weeks, sees their call resolved, sees the published analysis changed, and comes back. That is the whole brief in one screen.
Pilot fit
Essential — a two-month pilot lives or dies on week-3 retention.
low build riskretentionbundle with A1
A3

Minority Report — the dissent router

Consensus is the failure state. Surface the outlier as the most valuable object in the system.

81
projected
Thesis
The brief asks how to aggregate "without creating groupthink", and every team answers with an average. But a 5–10 person panel that converges in round one has stopped carrying information. The product should show spread, and route the dissenter to the analyst as the signal.
Why it scores
Concept scores high because it inverts the obvious answer and can be defended in one sentence. Visually distinctive — disagreement renders far better than agreement in a demo.
Build
Distribution + spread view → geographic disaggregation ("3 of 4 experts inside Chad disagree with 2 outside") → dissent queue for the moderator → escalation card with the dissenter's reasoning, still anonymous.
Hard part
Do not reward contrarianism. Dissent must be scored like everything else — a lone wrong outlier is just noise. Pair with A1's baseline so dissent is measured, not celebrated.
Demo moment
Seven experts, six say 80%. The seventh says 15% and is fully anonymous. The moderator sees only "one expert, fully anonymous, disagrees strongly — here is why." That is the trust model and the product in one frame.
Pilot fit
Strong. This is exactly the artefact ACAPS analysts want to receive.
mediummechanismbundle with A4
A4

Anonymity Budget — prove the negative

Anonymity as a computed guarantee, not a settings toggle. Show exactly what the aggregate can leak.

81
projected
Thesis
"Fully anonymous" is a promise; promises are cheap. Build the engine that computes whether a given contribution can be re-identified from what you publish — small-cell rules, cross-round differencing, attribute combinations — and blocks publication when it can.
Why it scores
Directly targets the 15% technology-feasibility criterion, where almost nobody competes. And it lets you demonstrate the hardest part of the brief live, on stage, in ten seconds.
Build
Policy engine (k-anonymity thresholds, suppression cells) → live "what can the public infer?" panel → automatic block with reason → audit trail of every suppression.
Hard part
Real anonymity needs an adversary model. State yours explicitly ("we defend against a motivated reader with all public outputs, not against ACAPS insiders") — an honest scoped claim beats an overreaching one.
Demo moment
Add a second expert to a cell and watch the system refuse to publish, explaining "1 of 2 experts in this cell is identifiable by elimination." Judges remember refusals.
Pilot fit
High — ACAPS will not put real experts on an unproven promise.
highsecuritybundle with A3
A5

Six-Month Clock — resolution infrastructure

The dataset has no dates, so no one can build a track record. Build the machine that fixes that.

80
projected
Thesis
Forecasting only becomes a track record when questions have explicit resolution criteria and dates. The export has 25 fields and not one timestamp — so make resolution a first-class product object: template, criterion, open date, resolve date, evidence, verdict.
Why it scores
Shows a depth of engagement with the supplied data that judges rarely see, and it is the enabling layer for any leaderboard claim. Concept is high; presentation is harder because the artefact is a workflow, not a graphic.
Build
Question generator from triggers → resolution-criteria editor ("the hazard occurred if ≥2 of these 3 sources report X by 30 Apr") → clock/queue → base-rate lookup pulled live from the 139 historical outcomes → resolution workflow with evidence capture.
Hard part
Resist over-engineering. Two templates ("trigger fires" and "exposure threshold crossed") cover the brief.
Demo moment
Point at a question and show the base rate: "risks like this materialised 84% of the time. Everything your panel says beyond that is the value they add."
Pilot fit
Very high. This is the piece ACAPS must own operationally after the pilot ends.
mediuminfrastructurebundle with A1
A6

Behind the Veil — moderator synthesis copilot

AI on the analyst's side of the glass. Turn nine free-text answers into a traceable draft rationale.

74
projected
Thesis
Both briefs permit AI behind the scenes and forbid it in the expert's face. So put it where the workload actually is: a moderator with nine paragraphs to reconcile before a Tuesday deadline.
Why it scores
Genuinely useful and on the right side of the "should not feel like a chatbot" rule. Ceiling is capped because it is a productivity layer — hard to demo as a concept, and Concept is 40%.
Build
Ingest contributions → cluster into consensus / contention / gaps → draft rationale in ACAPS house style → every sentence clickable back to the contribution that produced it → moderator edits and approves.
Hard part
Citation discipline. One uncited generated sentence and a jury member who knows the domain will discount the whole thing. Refuse to generate when unsupported.
Demo moment
Nine messy answers become a two-paragraph draft, and clicking any sentence highlights the exact expert text behind it.
Pilot fit
High value, but it is the moderator's tool — it does not itself retain experts.
mediumAIchatbot-adjacent
A7

Field Mode — the four-minute contribution

Offline-first PWA for Pierre: on a phone, on 2G, in French, between meetings.

71
projected
Thesis
Persona 03 is deployed for months with bad connectivity and doubts the effort pays off. Build for the worst device in the room, not the best screen in the demo.
Why it scores
Ticks the "could have" on mobile and low bandwidth and shows product maturity. But it is explicitly a could, and Concept is where the points are — a beautiful offline queue is still a queue.
Build
PWA with offline answer queue and background sync → sub-100 KB payload budget shown in the UI → French / Arabic / Spanish → SMS fallback for the lowest common denominator.
Hard part
It is deceptively hard to do well, and a half-working sync demos terribly. Only choose this with a real mobile engineer.
Demo moment
Airplane mode on, contribute two answers, airplane mode off, sync. Silence in the room for a second, then nodding.
Pilot fit
Decisive if ACAPS recruits field-based experts rather than desk analysts — worth asking them on Friday.

03 — ACAPS: the three worth actually picking

Safest high score
A1 + A2
Beat the Baseline with the Receipt loop. Trigger-level forecasting, scored against ACAPS's own completion rates, closed by a visible proof-of-impact. Concept strong, build genuinely finishable, and it maps one-to-one onto a December pilot. Pick this if you have one numerate person.
Highest concept ceiling
A3 + A4
Dissent router plus a provable anonymity engine. The deepest answer to the brief's two hardest words — groupthink and guarantee. Riskier to build; if the anonymity engine slips, fall back to A3 alone and present A4 as a specification. Pick this if you have a security-minded builder.
Best infrastructure story
A5 + A1
The Six-Month Clock makes A1's scores meaningful. Together they are a complete forecasting system with base rates, resolution rules and a track record. Harder to make visually exciting; wins on rigour. Pick this if your team likes boring things done properly.

My recommendation for ACAPS is A1 + A2, with A5 thinned to one screen. A1 gives you the concept, A2 gives you the story, and a single base-rate panel from A5 gives you the credibility. That is a complete arc — elicit, score, prove, retain — buildable by three people in one weekend, and it is the version most likely to survive contact with the ACAPS team on site.

04 — UNICRI: seven directions

The brief hands you the message and the help directory, then says the build is the integration. Every direction below is therefore about adoption, experience or trust — never detection.

The constraint that defines this track: the plugin contract already forbids the module from ever seeing the query text — it receives only type, severity, country, language, and a session-scoped ID that is never persisted. Every direction below must work inside that blindfold. Teams that break it to make a richer demo will fail the 15% ethics criterion on a single question.

U1

Live in a Day — the install path as product

One tag, one page, four minutes to a warning. Ship the copy-paste, not the architecture.

86
projected
Thesis
The challenge is stated as "a small team can switch on in days rather than months." So the product is not the warning — it is the time-to-live. Optimise the literal seconds between a moderator deciding and a warning being served.
Why it scores
Maximum alignment with the stated ask. The starter kit already runs, so prototype risk is low and polish is reachable. Directly convinces the 15% product-potential criterion.
Build
One-tag embed against the mock platform → Discourse + WordPress snippets → install page with a three-step wizard → a live install timer on the dashboard ("installed in 4 m 12 s") → portability proof from a second, unrelated page.
Hard part
Resisting scope. Every added format is a minute not spent on the install path.
Demo moment
Start the clock. Paste a snippet into a fresh page. Warning appears. Stop the clock at under five minutes.
Pilot fit
Direct — this is what a platform actually receives.
low build riskon-briefbundle with U7
U2

The Ladder — habituation as a solved problem

The brief asks what happens on the fifth sighting. Almost nobody will answer. Answer it.

84
projected
Thesis
A static interstitial trains people to dismiss it. Escalate instead: awareness → consequence → route to help → human handover, with hard caps, decay windows and rotation, so the fifth sighting is a different and more urgent intervention than the first.
Why it scores
Highest concept score available in this track, because it is a genuine mechanism that answers a question the brief explicitly poses. Very demoable — state changes are visible.
Build
Ladder state machine (session-scoped only) → repetition caps and cooling-off → variant rotation across the ladder → aggregate fatigue metrics → explicit "this person has seen 5 warnings" definition that still collects nothing identifying.
Hard part
State without identity. You cannot have persistent per-person escalation without tracking — so define the ladder over sessions and be transparent that this is a deliberate limit.
Demo moment
Trigger five times on the mock platform and watch the format escalate, then hard-cap and hand over to a human service.
Pilot fit
Strong — this is the part a small platform cannot build itself.
mediumexplicitly askedbundle with U5
U3

Honest Router — confidentiality without over-promising

Fear of being reported is the conversion killer. Tell the truth per country, including where the truth is bad.

82
projected
Thesis
The brief is unusual here: it insists you be honest about what is confidential, and notes England is moving the other way under the Crime and Policing Act 2026. A router that promises confidentiality where the law does not give it is a product that harms its user at the moment of maximum vulnerability.
Why it scores
Lands the 15% ethics/legal criterion harder than anything else on offer, and demonstrates the moral seriousness the topic demands. Concept is strong because it is counter-intuitive — the winning move is to sometimes not promise.
Build
Per-country legal + confidentiality config with sources → "what happens if you call" explainer written in plain language → audience routing (self / partner / family / professional) → visible [VERIFY] markers wherever a claim is unconfirmed.
Hard part
You must not invent law. Use the provided legal-confidentiality.json and cite every assertion; where the file is silent, show silence.
Demo moment
The same click, routed to NL and to England, producing honestly different promises — and the England version telling the user plainly that confidentiality is not guaranteed.
Pilot fit
Essential for any EU roll-out, which is exactly what the brief asks to scale toward.
mediumlegalbundle with U4
U4

Blind Measurement — impact without watching people

Prove the warning works at n=900, where dashboards are structurally blind. Statistics as a feature.

81
projected
Thesis
At a 1% click-through rate you need ~4,600 impressions to detect a doubling, and counts under 10 are suppressed by contract. So the honest dashboard reports exposure immediately, direction after weeks, and significance almost never. Build for that reality instead of faking precision.
Why it scores
Highest technical-feasibility ceiling in the track. Almost no team will attempt it, and the ones that do usually produce a vanity chart that a statistically literate judge dismantles in one question.
Build
Aggregate counter service with suppression at 10 → Bayesian credible intervals rather than point rates → exposure/outcome separation → sequential testing so a platform can stop early → a plain-language verdict line ("no signal yet; expect one around week 6").
Hard part
Making an honest dashboard feel like a product. A screen that says "not enough data" is correct and boring — pair it with the exposure curve, which is always moving and always true.
Demo moment
Show a rival-style dashboard and then yours side by side: theirs reports "1.2% conversion" from 83 impressions; yours refuses, and says why.
Pilot fit
Directly answers "how does a platform know it works without tracking individuals" — a named guiding question.
highstatisticsbundle with U3
U5

Message Lab — variants with evidence attached

Every message block carries its citation. Build the registry, the assignment harness and the translation guardrails.

77
projected
Thesis
The brief supplies NL/EN blocks with sources and asks you to "design for testing variants". Turn the message from copy into a governed asset: tone (deterrent / therapeutic / mixed), reading level, evidence link, and a guardrail that refuses to machine-translate an unverified legal claim.
Why it scores
Solid on product potential and shows evidentiary rigour. Capped because the brief gives you the messages — refining supplied content reads as polish rather than invention.
Build
Block registry with evidence → variant assignment harness → results view (respecting suppression) → translation pipeline with [VERIFY] enforcement on legal claims → readability scoring.
Hard part
Staying in your own words, per SAFETY.md rule 6. Copying Lucy Faithfull or Protect Children wording is an immediate credibility loss.
Demo moment
Take one block, show its evidence, show a second tone variant, show the assignment, show the guardrail refusing to ship an unverified Dutch-to-German legal translation.
Pilot fit
Good — variant governance is what makes multi-country scale-up safe.

U6

The Jump — obsess the one-click handover

1 in 100 click through. Fix the handover, not the warning.

74
projected
Thesis
The brief names the drop-off explicitly: roughly 1 in 100 clicks through to help, and "this jump is where most people drop off". So build the jump as the product — no login, no form, no ambiguity, prefilled context, and a clear picture of what happens next.
Why it scores
Narrow and very demoable, with excellent accessibility and trust detail. Capped because it is one screen — it cannot carry a 40% concept score alone.
Build
One-tap to Preventielijn van Offlimits and Stoppen is mogelijk → "what to expect on the call" pre-brief → return path after the handover → screen-reader and contrast pass → plain-language Dutch and English.
Hard part
It is small. Bundle it into U1 or U2 or it under-fills the weekend.
Demo moment
From warning to a person, in one tap, with the confidentiality statement visible before the tap and not after.
Pilot fit
Every other direction depends on it, which makes it a good thing to own even as a component.

U7

The Yes — a decision kit for the part-time moderator

The buyer fears legal risk and user trust. Build the artefact that gets a yes in one meeting.

80
projected
Thesis
The guiding question is literally "what would a trust and safety lead at a small platform need to say yes to installing this tomorrow?" Answer it as a product: a data-flow statement, an obligations checklist, a sandbox mode, and a one-page sign-off.
Why it scores
On-brief, and it activates the 5% business criterion that most teams will waste. High product-potential score because adoption friction is the market barrier.
Build
Install wizard → data-flow diagram generated from the actual contract (not hand-drawn) → DSA / Dutch-law obligations checklist → sandbox mode with synthetic traffic → one-page decision memo a non-technical moderator can sign.
Hard part
Never overstate legal compliance. Frame as "questions to put to your lawyer", not "you are compliant".
Demo moment
Hand the judge the one-page sign-off and say: "this is what the moderator reads before they say yes."
Pilot fit
High — adoption is the bottleneck the whole challenge exists to solve.

05 — UNICRI: the three worth actually picking

Safest high score
U1 + U7
Install path plus the decision kit. Answers "days not months" with a stopwatch and answers "why would a moderator say yes" with a document. Build risk is low because the starter kit already runs. Pick this if your team ships front-end well.
Highest concept ceiling
U2 + U5
The habituation ladder with an evidence-governed message registry. Directly answers the fifth-sighting question and the "design for variants" instruction in one coherent experience layer. Pick this if you have a strong interaction designer.
Most defensible
U3 + U4
Honest confidentiality routing plus blind measurement. The two things a serious NGO will interrogate, answered with rigour rather than enthusiasm. Harder to make exciting, easiest to defend under questioning. Pick this if you have a statistician or a lawyer.

07 — The choice, in one table

Pick by team shape rather than by preference. The direction you can staff is the direction that scores.

If your strongest person is…ChooseBecause
Numerate — comfortable with Brier scores and base ratesACAPS A1 + A2The dataset is the entire moat. Nobody else will bring calibration math, and it converts directly into a pilot.
Front-end / design — ships fast, cares about the feelUNICRI U1 + U7The starter kit already runs, so your hours buy polish and persuasion, not plumbing.
Interaction designer — thinks in states and edge casesUNICRI U2 + U5Habituation ladders are a state-machine problem with a visible payoff.
Security / backendACAPS A4, fallback A3"Prove the negative" is the only place in either brief where real systems thinking wins outright.
Lawyer, policy or NGO personUNICRI U3 + U4Confidentiality and measurement honesty are domain problems before they are code problems.
Writer or storytellerACAPS A2 on top of A1The receipt loop is a narrative artefact; it needs someone who can write a resolution screen that makes someone come back.
Generalist 3-person teamACAPS A1 + A2 (thinned A5)One screen, one score, one loop. The least coordination overhead of any high-scoring combination.

The composition rule

Take one direction that owns the concept and one that owns the proof. Never two that own the concept — you will build half of each. The proven pairings are A1+A2 (elicit + retain), A3+A4 (invert + guarantee), U1+U7 (ship + persuade), U2+U5 (experience + evidence), U3+U4 (promise + measure).

If you can only finish one, finish the proof one — a working receipt loop with a modest elicitation beats an elegant elicitation with a broken loop, because judges grade what runs.

You have roughly 26 on-site hours plus one night of asynchronous work. Allocate by role, and protect the last six hours for the demo path only.

3-person team

Builder + designer/builder + one person who does both the numbers and the pitch. That last role is the constraint — protect it. Cut the moderator view before you cut the evidence chart.

5-person team

Your risk is coordination, not capacity. Split into experience and evidence pairs with one integrator, and enforce a single 15-minute standup at 09:00 and 18:00. Do not let anyone work on a surface that is not on the demo path.

09 — Milestone gates

Hard checkpoints. If a gate is missed, cut scope immediately rather than compressing the next phase.

Gate 1 — Fri 18:00: the skeleton walks

Entry → one question → stored answer → aggregate displayed. Ugly is fine. If this is not running, stop adding features and fix it.

Gate 2 — Sat 12:30: the mechanism exists

The thing that makes you different — the receipt, the ladder, the anonymity block — is real, not mocked. This is the gate that decides your score.

Gate 3 — Sat 16:00: the pitch has been performed

Pitch practice is a free slot with organisers. Teams that skip it deliver a cold 3-minute pitch on Sunday. Do not be that team.

Gate 4 — Sun 08:00: feature freeze

Nothing new after this. Only the demo path, the numbers, and the words. Any new feature now is a bug you have not met yet.

Gate 5 — Sun 10:30: submitted

Repo, description, demo link. The real cut-off is 11:00, not the 12:00 printed on the schedule.

10 — Questions to ask on Friday

Challenge deep dives are at 14:00 Friday. These are the questions that change architecture, not small talk. Ask two, listen hard, adjust by 15:00.

For ACAPS

  1. "What does Completion rate mean on a trigger — your confidence it will fire, or progress toward it?" The entire A1 direction depends on the answer, and it decides whether your scoring model is valid or nonsense.
  2. "When a risk materialises, who records the date, and where does it live?" If the answer is "in a spreadsheet we keep internally", A5 just became the pilot-critical direction.
  3. "Seven experts in Chad — are they desk-based in N'Djamena or field-deployed?" Decides whether A7 (Field Mode) is a nice-to-have or the whole product.
  4. "What did the last panel get wrong, and did anyone tell the experts?" If the answer is "nobody told them", A2 (the Receipt) is not a feature, it is the fix.

For UNICRI

  1. "Has any small platform actually installed a warning, and what stopped the ones that didn't?" This is the entire challenge in one answer — and it tells you whether to build U1 or U7 first.
  2. "When a platform's own lawyer looks at this, what is the first thing they ask?" Answer that on the install page and you beat every team that did not ask.
  3. "Do you want the fifth-sighting behaviour to escalate, or to stop showing entirely?" Both are defensible; guessing wrong makes your ladder a liability. Ask.
  4. "Is a plausible-but-unvalidated impact model acceptable, or would you rather see 'no data yet'?" If they say the latter, U4 is the direction and you should start it tonight.

11 — What not to build

Do not buildWhy
A general survey toolBoth briefs describe a loop with feedback and resolution. A form with a chart is the one thing every team will produce.
A superforecaster leaderboard on invented dataYou cannot compute a real track record from the export — there are no dates. Fake numbers are a single question away from collapse.
CSAM detection (UNICRI)Forbidden in a full paragraph of the brief. Detection exists; distribution is the gap.
A chatbot as the expert-facing surface (ACAPS)Explicitly on the "should not" list. AI belongs behind the moderator or it costs you points.
An anonymity settings dropdownThree tiers is the floor, not the feature. Without a computed guarantee you have built a preference, not a protection.
Two directions at full depthThe most common way good teams lose. One owns the concept, one owns the proof. Nothing else runs.
A business model slide with a market size5% of the rubric, and neither customer pays. One sentence: who signs, what it costs to run.

If you read only one thing: pick A1 + A2 if you have anyone numerate, or U1 + U7 if you ship front-end fast. Both are 86-point ceilings with low build risk, both answer a question the brief actually asks, and both produce a demo that runs. Then spend Saturday afternoon on the one mechanism that makes it yours — the receipt, or the stopwatch — and Sunday morning on the words.
